# Personal narrative (LinkedIn post, blog post, a story about Justin)

A story Justin tells about himself to people who don't know him. It shares the Slack register's flow (`slack.md`: written the way he'd say it out loud) and differs in everything else. The subject is him and not a decision, the reader is a stranger and not a teammate, and the content is people, feelings, and what happened, in order.

**His raw dictation is the voice sample.** When Justin tells the story in chat, by voice or fast typing, that text is the highest signal there is for word choice, point of view, and how he links one thought to the next. Clean it up (typos, repeats, order) and keep the vocabulary and the sentence shape. The final draft reads like the dictation on a good day, not like a rewrite of it. No dictation yet → ask him to tell the story first, then interview for the gaps.

What it sounds like:

> although there are very very hard problems at ec2. there are a lot of engineering problems and scaling problems. whereas here i'm dealing with like [...] figuring out what's a good direction to go and there's no right or wrong. there's just improvements.
>
> it's been probably some of the funnest, exciting times of my life. it's honestly everything i hope for and more.

- **First person, in story order.** "I ... and then I ...". Past tense for what happened, present for how it is now, and present for people who are still there ("they are a great team").
- **Long sentences, joined with the words he uses out loud**: and, so, but, because, although, whereas, which. One thought leads into the next. The test for any line is whether he'd say it to a friend across a table. A line built for effect fails it: a one-line paragraph that lands a point, a colon reveal, a setup-and-payoff pair ("The wanting was the easy part").
- **Everyday words, plain intensifiers.** "really hard", "a lot of", "a bunch of", "honestly", "I think", "I guess". Emphasis comes from "really" and "very", where a vivid adjective or a metaphor would read as writing. Common idioms he already uses are his ("in the same boat", "leap of faith", "an itch", "found a groove"); a phrase coined for the post is not.
- **Feelings stated flat, and only the ones he stated.** He names the feeling and moves on; a vulnerable detail can sit in a light aside ("(and some crying too)"). Genre research decides structure and length, and it never adds a feeling, doubt, or opinion he didn't express. When he's sure, the draft is sure: a hedge he doesn't feel is as wrong as a boast.
- **Facts over labels.** The specific fact ("promoted to L5") replaces the vague one plus what it implies ("promoted, with a pay bump"). He says where he sat or what he did ("I was several layers away from the customer") without first labeling himself ("I was a builder who...").
- **Generous to people.** He names people by relationship, thanks the team he left in plain words, and shares the blame ("we usually only call each other", not "she only calls me"). Anything that could put a friend or a former employer in an awkward spot stays out, even when it makes the story better. Confirm names with him before they go in.
- **Work explained by contrast and by the questions he works on.** "There the hard problems were X, whereas here..."; "how do you know it's getting better?". One concrete example carries it.
- **Ends as an invitation**, one or two sentences, wide enough for anyone curious. No hashtags.

The spoken voice costs words, and he will pay that cost over a platform's ideal length. Say once that a draft is over, name the safest cut, and leave it.

**The first full draft goes to him through `mdnote wait`** (the `mdnote` skill). Line-level notes converged in four short rounds; drafts pasted into the terminal got one broad correction each.

## Worked example

The draft that came back as "hard to read" and mannered:

> The wanting was the easy part.
>
> There were a lot of conversations and a lot of crying. What got me unstuck was the worst case: it doesn't work out, and I move back. That's not that bad.

What he approved:

> I'd known for a long time that this is what I wanted, but when it came to actually deciding, there were a lot of long conversations (and some crying too).
>
> What helped in the end was thinking about the worst case, which is that if it doesn't work out, I move back to Vancouver, and that's really not that bad.

Same facts. The first is four short sentences arranged for rhythm, with a fragment and a colon doing the work. The second is two sentences he could say out loud, joined with "but", "which is that", and "and", and nearly every phrase in it ("when it came to actually", "not that bad") is from his dictation.
